How to make screen time rules that actually stick

Pediatricians no longer give one daily hour limit for school-age kids. Here's what they focus on instead, and how to build a plan your family will follow.

By the OurDot team · Updated October 24, 2026

The short answer

The AAP's January 2026 guidance focuses on what kids do on screens, when, and what it replaces (sleep, play, homework, family time), rather than one hour count. For under-5s, keep screens minimal under 18 months (video chats with family are fine) and about an hour of quality content a day for ages 2 to 5. Plans stick best when you make them with your kids, pair a few clear limits with regular conversation, and follow them yourself.

Two federal-level voices disagree

The AAP (January 2026) moved away from fixed limits for older kids. A May 2026 warning from the Surgeon General's office recommends no more than 2 hours a day for ages 6 to 18, under an hour for under-6s, and no screens under 18 months. If you see "2 hours a day," it comes from the 2026 Surgeon General's-office warning, not the AAP.

What research says works

  • Talk, don't just restrict. A meta-analysis of 52 studies (Chen & Shi, 2019) found restrictive rules were better at cutting time, while talking with kids about media and using it together were better at reducing media-related risks.
  • Model it. Research by Brandon McDaniel and Jenny Radesky links parents' device interruptions with more child behavior problems over time, and the relationship seems to run both ways.
  • Protect sleep. Keeping devices out of bedrooms at night is advice shared by the AAP, the UK's Chief Medical Officers and the 2023 Surgeon General's advisory. Why sleep matters most.

Seven steps to a plan that sticks

  1. Make it together.

    Hold a family meeting and fill in the AAP's free Family Media Plan.

  2. Set where-and-when rules.

    No devices at meals or in bedrooms overnight is easier to follow than a minute count.

  3. Name what screens can't crowd out.

    Sleep, homework, movement and time together.

  4. Keep talking.

    Ask what they watched and who they talked to, with curiosity, not interrogation.

  5. Use controls as training wheels.

    Loosen them as kids show they're ready, as the APA recommends.

  6. Grown-ups follow the rules too.

    Kids notice.

  7. Review every few months.

    What worked? What needs to change as they grow?
